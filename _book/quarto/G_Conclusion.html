8  Discussion & Conclusion

The present study combined literature datasets and new density-dependent experiments to develop a mechanistic DEB model for A. caliginosa linking growth, maturation, and reproduction to food availability and population density. By extending previous experiments to the juvenile stage under different density conditions, this work provides new insights into the mechanisms underlying density dependence in earthworms and their representation within a DEB framework.

8.1 Resource sharing and apparent density effects on growth and reproduction

The density experiment confirmed that increasing the number of individuals per cosm reduced juvenile growth, adult body mass, and reproductive output. However, because all microcosms received the same total amount of food irrespective of the number of individuals, increasing density simultaneously reduced food availability per individual. Under these experimental conditions, the observed reduction in performance therefore reflects primarily a resource-sharing effect rather than density dependence sensu stricto.

Consistent with this interpretation, the DEB model reproduced juvenile growth without an explicit density-dependent process, as treatment differences emerged from variation in per-capita access to organic matter. Model simulations further supported this interpretation, showing lower food availability and functional response at higher densities (?fig-OMdynFood & Figure 6.66), and indicating that differences in resource acquisition explained much of the variation in growth and maturation among treatments. However, the predicted-observed analysis also showed that fit quality decreased under high juvenile densities and low per-capita food availability, with negative residuals indicating an underestimation of juvenile body mass in the most limiting conditions. This suggests that, although resource sharing explains the main density-related patterns, the assumption of homogeneous resource partitioning among individuals may become limiting at high density. This interpretation is particularly relevant at the highest tested population density (7 individuals per cosm), where one consistently smaller individual per replicate eventually died. Because the model assumes equal resource sharing, it may have underestimated the food available to the surviving worms and, consequently, their juvenile growth. This illustrates that, under high-density conditions, resource-mediated effects may interact with individual heterogeneity and asymmetric competition.

This result differs somewhat from previous earthworm studies that reported density-dependent effects even under apparently non-limiting food conditions. For example, Klok (2007) observed delayed maturation and reduced cocoon production at high densities despite surplus food availability and interpreted these effects as reduced ingestion caused by spatial interference among individuals. Similarly, Rakel et al. (2020) reproduced density-related reductions in growth in E. fetida through crowding effects affecting food acquisition. In contrast, the present results indicate that much of the variation observed here can be explained without invoking an explicit crowding effect, suggesting that reduced access to food was the dominant mechanism under our experimental conditions.

The comparison between D2 and D2b provides additional support for this interpretation. Doubling food input at density 2 produced only limited increases in growth and reproduction despite substantially greater horse dung availability. Model simulations indicate that the functional response in D2 was already close to saturation (\(f \approx 1\)), implying that additional food yielded little energetic benefit. Under these conditions, D2 may already approximate ad libitum feeding, making D2b of limited physiological benefit despite greater food supply. This result suggests that, once food acquisition approaches saturation, increasing organic matter input does not necessarily translate into greater assimilation and may instead simply alter the physical or microbial environment of the cosm. From a practical perspective, these results also suggest that standard feeding conditions similar to D2 may already be sufficient to maintain near-maximal growth and reproduction, while requiring substantially less horse dung.

Although the present design does not allow complete separation of resource-mediated and density-dependent effects, it highlights the importance of explicitly accounting for per-capita food availability when interpreting earthworm density experiments. Future experiments manipulating density while maintaining equivalent per-capita food conditions (e.g., a high-density treatment with ad libitum food availability) would help determine whether crowding effects independent of resource limitation emerge in A. caliginosa.

The density experiments conducted here also improve the estimation of age at maturity compared with previous experimental designs. In Bart et al. (2020), individuals were isolated during the juvenile stage and paired only once adulthood was visually detected. Because maturity checks occurred at discrete time intervals, the true age at maturity may have been overestimated by up to one observation interval. In contrast, earthworms in the present density experiment were maintained in groups from the beginning of the experiment, ensuring continuous reproductive opportunity once maturity was reached. This design removes the observational delay between physiological maturation and effective reproduction and therefore provides more reliable data for calibrating life-history transitions within the DEB model.

8.2 Energy assimilation and functional response

A major objective of this study was to mechanistically relate the functional response (\(f\)) to the quantity and quality of organic matter available in the environment. Unlike several previous earthworm DEB studies such as Rakel et al. (2020) and Gergs et al. (2022), where the functional response was estimated independently for each experiment, the present model explicitly links assimilated energy to measurable environmental variables, namely the amounts of horse dung and soil organic matter present in the cosm.

The model comparison strongly supported formulations using a normalized hyperbolic function relating ingestion to the functional response. In classical hyperbolic formulations (David et al., 2018; Gergs et al., 2022; Kooijman, 2009), the functional response asymptotically approaches 1 but never reaches it even when ingestion reaches its maximum value. The normalized formulation avoids this inconsistency by constraining the function between 0 and 1 while maintaining a flexible curvature through parameter \(K\).

The results also support a strong difference in energetic quality between horse dung and soil organic matter. The low estimated value of \(r_{OM}\) (0.02) indicates that soil organic matter contributed little to assimilated energy relative to horse dung, consistent with the negligible growth observed under soil-only conditions in Bart2019_XP3. Such low energetic contribution of soil organic matter likely reflects the limited accessibility and digestibility of native soil carbon relative to fresh organic amendments. Differences in biochemical quality and decomposability among organic matter sources are well documented in soils, with fresh organic amendments generally providing more labile and energetically accessible compounds than stabilized soil organic matter (Lorenz et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, quantifying ingestion and assimilation in endogeic earthworms remains particularly difficult because these organisms live directly within their food matrix. Unlike aquatic or surface-feeding organisms, ingestion cannot easily be measured without disturbing the animals and modifying their behaviour. Consequently, most available estimates rely on indirect approaches such as defecation rates, gut transit measurements, or nitrogen assimilation efficiencies. For example, Barley (1959) showed that A. caliginosa may process substantial quantities of soil relative to its body mass, with smaller individuals ingesting proportionally more material than larger ones. Similarly, Whalen & Parmelee (1999) estimated organic matter consumption indirectly from nitrogen assimilation efficiencies, while Taylor & Taylor (2014) used fungal spores as natural tracers to estimate gut transit time and daily egestion rates. Together, these studies illustrate that energy acquisition in earthworms results from multiple interacting processes, including burrowing activity, ingestion, digestion, gut transit, and assimilation efficiency. Consequently, the effective acquisition of assimilable energy may not scale solely with structural surface area.

Experiment Bart2019_XP3 was particularly informative in this regard because earthworms were alternately maintained on soil alone and on soil supplemented with horse dung. This experimental condition generated strong temporal variations in both the quantity and quality of available organic matter, providing a stringent test of how food acquisition should scale with body size under changing feeding conditions. Under these circumstances, the formulation in which the maximum searching rate scaled with structural mass provided a better fit than the formulation based on structural surface area. However, this result should be interpreted cautiously. The estimated value of \([\dot{F}_m]\) reached approximately 7 \(g_{soil}.d\)^{-1}\(.g\)_{worm}{-1}\(, which is far above available estimates of soil processing rates for *A. caliginosa*. For instance, @barley_1959 reported defecation rates of approximately 0.32 g dry faeces g\){-1}$ fresh weight d\(^{-1}\) in small individuals and 0.22 g dry faeces g\(^{-1}\) fresh weight d\(^{-1}\) in larger individuals. Interpreted literally, the estimated \([\dot{F}_m]\) would therefore imply unrealistically high soil ingestion rates. This discrepancy suggests that \([\dot{F}_m]\) should not be interpreted as a direct measure of ingestion capacity, but rather as an effective parameter compensating for simplified representation of food acquisition and assimilation. In the current formulation, ingestion is assumed to depend on organic matter density in the cosm, but earthworms may selectively ingest organic-rich microsites, mucus-enriched soil, microbial biomass, or partially decomposed dung particles rather than processing soil homogenously. In addition, the energetic value of horse dung was fixed, although its actual assimilable energy may vary with decomposition state and microbial activity.

Compared with Gergs et al. (2022), the present model produced parameter estimates that appear more consistent with earthworm physiology and the amount of energy available in the environment from soil organic matter and horse dung. In particular, the lower estimates of \(\{\dot p_{Am}\}\) and \([\dot F_m]\) reduced the unrealistically high energy inputs implied by the previous formulation, while estimated structural density (\(d_V = 1.2 \mathrm{g.cm}^{-3}\)) was more compatible with earthworm tissue density than the value of 0.27 \(\mathrm{g.cm}^{-3}\) reported in Gergs et al. (2022). Their parametrization also implies a much higher initial egg reserve of 131 J per cocoon, compared with about 68.4 J in the present model. Together, these differences suggest that the formulation of Gergs et al. (2022) relies on substantially larger energy fluxes than those supported by available physiological and environmental information.

Parameter compensation and identifiability issues are common in DEB model calibration, particularly for ingestion- and assimilation-related parameters that integrate multiple physiological processes (Lika & Kooijman, 2011). In the present model, this issue is particularly relevant for the parameters governing energy acquisition. The digestion efficiency (\(\kappa_X\)) was fixed based on nitrogen assimilation efficiencies reported by Whalen & Parmelee (1999), but this value remains uncertain and may vary with the type and quality of ingested organic matter. Moreover, \(\kappa_X\) is mathematically closely linked to the maximum searching rate (\([\dot{F}_m]\)), since both parameters determine the amount of energy ultimately assimilated from ingested material. As a result, these parameters may partly compensate each other during calibration.

Consistent with this interpretation, a strong posterior correlation was observed between \([\dot{F}_m]\) and \(K\) (Figure 6.28), indicating that the maximum searching rate and the curvature of the functional response also partly compensate each other. The condition-specific biases observed across feeding treatments further indicate that the relationship between organic matter availability and assimilated energy remains imperfectly constrained. In particular, deviations were not strictly proportional to the amount of food supplied, suggesting that food accessibility, selection, and digestive efficiency may be as important as food quantity itself.

Together, these results suggest that \([\dot{F}_m]\), \(\kappa_X\), and \(K\) should be interpreted cautiously as effective parameters describing overall energy acquisition rather than as independent direct measurements of ingestion, digestion, or assimilation processes. This highlights the need for additional experimental data on ingestion, egestion, gut transit time, organic matter selection, and digestion efficiency to better constrain the mechanistic link between environmental organic matter and assimilated energy. The sensitivity analysis further supported this interpretation. While individual-level endpoints were only weakly sensitive to most ingestion-related parameters under the selected scenario, the amount of organic matter remaining in the cosm was influenced by a broader set of parameters, particularly \([\dot{F}_m]\), \(\kappa_X\), \(\mu_{OM}\), and \(d_V\). This indicates that environmental resource dynamics integrate several interacting processes related to ingestion, digestion, organic matter transformation, and individual growth. It also confirms that these processes are difficult to disentangle using body-mass and reproduction data alone.

The present results also suggest that reserve contribution to fresh body mass is relatively very limited in A. caliginosa. The formulation assuming reserve mass independent of structural size consistently outperformed alternatives where reserve contribution scaled proportionally with structure. Similar formulations have been used in other DEB applications to better represent body mass dynamics under variable feeding conditions (Dortel et al., 2020). In addition, experiment Bart2019_XP3 showed little decrease in fresh body mass during periods where reserves were expected to decline, suggesting that reserve depletion contributes only weakly to total wet mass under the tested conditions.

8.3 Energy allocation and the \(\kappa\) rule

The model comparison strongly supported formulations including a progressive transition in energy allocation after maturation. Models assuming a fixed allocation rule throughout the life cycle systematically produced poorer fits and resulted in unrealistic estimates for the energy content of cocoons. In contrast, introducing a gradual transition in allocation over a characteristic timescale \(\tau\) substantially improved model performance.

These results provide an intermediate interpretation between the standard DEB \(\kappa\)-rule and alternative energy-budget formulations proposed for earthworms. Johnston et al. (2014) argued that reproduction should take priority over growth in adults and therefore rejected the standard DEB assumption of a constant allocation fraction. In contrast, Rakel et al. (2020) showed that the standard DEB model could adequately reproduce several experimental datasets when density effects were interpreted as competition for food and crowding rather than as changes in allocation strategy. More recently, Gergs et al. (2022) reproduced the growth plateau observed when adults were grouped by introducing different functional response values between juvenile and grouped-adult phases, effectively modifying energy assimilation after grouping.

Our results suggest that neither a strictly constant nor an instantaneous allocation shift fully captures the observed dynamics. The progressive transition introduced here allowed the model to better reproduce the smoother growth trajectories observed in the new density experiments, where juveniles were maintained in groups from the beginning of the experiment. In contrast, experiments from Bart (2019) displayed a sharper transition between juvenile growth and adult plateau, likely because adults were grouped only after maturity had been detected. Importantly, in these experiments, individuals experience a transient period during which they are physiologically mature but still isolated, because pairing occurs only after the experimenter identifies adulthood. During this interval, worms are effectively in an “isolated adult” state, with reproductive opportunities still absent. In the present model, this situation is represented by maintaining the juvenile allocation fraction (\(\kappa_J\)) until grouping occurs, after which a progressive transition toward the adult allocation fraction (\(\kappa_A\)) is initiated.

More generally, these results are consistent with the view that energy allocation may shift during ontogeny as organisms transition from growth-dominated juvenile stages to reproductive adult stages. Such stage-dependent allocation patterns have been documented in other organisms, for instance in Daphnia magna, where age affects the balance between somatic growth and reproductive investment (Glazier & Calow, 1992). These shifts also fit within the broader framework of life-history trade-offs, in which organisms allocate limited resources among competing functions such as growth, maintenance, and reproduction (Zera & Harshman, 2001). In A. caliginosa, the progressive transition in \(\kappa\) may therefore reflect both physiological changes associated with maturation and delays associated with the establishment of reproductive interactions.

The sensitivity analysis further showed that the representation of energy allocation is a major determinant of model behaviour. When temperature-related parameters were excluded, (\(\kappa_J\)) was the main driver of body mass, maturity, and reproduction at both juvenile and adult stages. Its influence across several endpoints indicates that uncertainty in juvenile allocation propagates throughout the life cycle, including after the transition toward the adult allocation strategy. This result supports the importance of accurately constraining allocation parameters, while also showing that several life-history predictions depend on a common underlying allocation mechanism.

Simulations of isolated adults nevertheless highlighted limitations in the current representation of post-maturation energy allocation. The growth trajectories observed for isolated adults suggest that these individuals occupy an intermediate physiological state between juveniles and grouped reproducing adults. Maintaining the juvenile allocation strategy throughout adulthood resulted in unrealistic continuous growth, whereas reallocating energy toward reproduction as in grouped adults produced growth plateaus that were too pronounced. These results indicate that isolated adults cannot be fully described either as juveniles or as reproductively active adults within the current DEB formulations. Additionally, this formulation still predicted a non-zero reproductive flux after maturation despite the absence of observed cocoon production.

This discrepancy raises the question of the energetic fate of reproduction-related fluxes in isolated individuals. One possible interpretation is that isolated adults still undergo physiological changes associated with maturation, including maturity maintenance costs, but that the energy normally allocated to reproduction is partly redirected toward somatic growth or reserve dynamics in the absence of mating opportunities. Such a mechanism could explain why isolated adults continue growing after maturation. More generally, these results suggest that isolated adults represent a distinct physiological state that is not fully captured by the current DEB formulations.

However, the ecological relevance of isolated adults remains limited. Under natural conditions, complete reproductive isolation is likely rare except at very low population densities. Consequently, the isolated-adult experiments should primarily be viewed as a useful conceptual framework for exploring energy allocation dynamics rather than as a realistic representation of field conditions.

8.4 Model limitations and perspectives

Despite generally good agreement between simulations and observations, some systematic discrepancies remained. Growth plateaus were under or overestimated in some grouped adult experiments, and the transition between juvenile and adult growth remained more abrupt in several density treatments than in observations. The predicted-observed analyses further indicated that fit quality decreased under high juvenile densities and low per-capita food availability, with negative residuals suggesting an underestimation of juvenile body mass under the most limiting conditions (Figure 4). These patterns indicate that deviations were not randomly distributed across datasets, but were associated with specific combinations of density, food availability, developmental stage, and experimental context.

The model also did not reproduce the reduction in reproductive output observed for grouped individuals under low-food conditions (Bart2019_XP4_2) when density increased from three to six individuals per cosm under non-ad libitum feeding conditions. However, this effect was observed in only a single experimental condition, preventing robust incorporation of an explicit density-dependent reproductive process into the model. Reproductive output was also overestimated for individuals at density 2 with high food conditions (Gollot2026_D2b), where observed reproduction remained lower than predicted despite high food availability. This suggests that increasing food availability does not necessarily translate into proportional increases in reproduction and that additional processes, such as reproductive timing, individual variability, or microenvironmental effects, may influence cocoon production. As discussed above, the underestimation of juvenile growth at high population densities suggests that the assumption of homogeneous resource sharing may be too simplistic.

Overall, these discrepancies likely reflect processes that are not explicitly represented in the current formulation. First, effects of density may not be exclusively mediated by resource limitation. Behavioural interactions, spatial interference, or asymmetric competition may influence ingestion and reproduction. Second, variability among individuals in maturation timing may smooth the population-level transition between juvenile and adult stages, whereas the current model assumes homogeneous transition dynamics. Third, the quantification of ingestion and assimilation processes in endogeic earthworms remains highly uncertain because individuals feed directly within the soil matrix. Consequently, the ingestion-related parameters likely integrate multiple processes, including soil exploration, ingestion, digestion, gut transit, and assimilation efficiency. The sensitivity analysis also highlighted the strong influence of temperature correction on model predictions. When thermal parameters were included, \(T_{\mathrm{ref}}\) and \(T_H\) dominated the sensitivity of most endpoints, and total-order indices were frequently much larger than first-order indices. This indicates substantial interactions between temperature correction, energy allocation, and other physiological processes. Accurate characterization of thermal dependence is therefore essential before applying the model across variable temperature regimes. However, because \(T_{\mathrm{ref}}\) acts as a reference value in the correction of all physiological rates, its dominant sensitivity partly reflects its structural role in the model rather than the influence of a single biological process.

Future work should therefore focus on improving the mechanistic representation of energy acquisition and the thermal dependence of physiological rates in earthworms. In particular, experiments manipulating both the quantity and energetic quality of organic matter sources would help disentangle the respective roles of food quantity, food quality, and resource accessibility. Characterizing the energetic value of different organic amendments and soil organic matter fractions could further improve the mechanistic representation of assimilation processes and the estimation of functional response parameters. Simultaneous monitoring of organic matter depletion, ingestion, defecation, and cocoon production would also provide valuable constraints for assimilation-related parameters and the functional response.

8.5 Conclusion

This study developed a Dynamic Energy Budget model for Aporrectodea caliginosa integrating the effects of food availability, organic matter quality, and population density on growth, maturation, and reproduction. By combining literature datasets with new density experiments performed from the juvenile stage onward, the model was calibrated across a broader range of life stages and environmental conditions than previous earthworm DEB studies.

The results indicate that reduced juvenile growth at higher densities can largely be explained by lower per-capita resource availability and food acquisition, without requiring an explicit density-dependent effect on energy assimilation. In contrast, the best-performing formulations also showed that post-maturation energy allocation cannot be adequately described by a strictly constant allocation rule throughout the life cycle. Introducing a progressive transition in energy allocation after maturation substantially improved the description of adult growth and reproduction trajectories, suggesting that reproductive opportunities shape energetic allocation in adult earthworms.

A major contribution of this work is the mechanistic linkage established between the functional response and environmental organic matter availability. The model explicitly distinguishes the energetic contribution of horse dung and soil organic matter, highlighting the limited assimilable energetic value of native soil organic matter relative to fresh organic amendments. The study also emphasizes the methodological difficulties associated with quantifying ingestion and assimilation processes in endogeic earthworms, which remain important limitations for mechanistic modelling approaches.

Overall, the proposed framework improves the ecological realism of DEB modelling for soil invertebrates by strengthening the links between environmental variability and individual energetics. This work provides a robust basis for future developments integrating multiple stressors, including pesticides and climate-related constraints, within DEB-TKTD modelling approaches for earthworms.

Consequently, the effective acquisition of assimilable energy may not scale solely with structural surface area.

Experiment Bart2019_XP3 was particularly informative in this regard because earthworms were alternately maintained on soil alone and on soil supplemented with horse dung. This experimental condition generated strong temporal variations in both the quantity and quality of available organic matter, providing a stringent test of how food acquisition should scale with body size under changing feeding conditions. Under these circumstances, the formulation in which the maximum searching rate scaled with structural mass provided a better fit than the formulation based on structural surface area. However, this result should be interpreted cautiously. The estimated value of $[\dot{F}_m]$ reached approximately 7 $g_{soil}.d$\^{-1}$.g$\_{worm}^{-1}^$, which is far above available estimates of soil processing rates for *A. caliginosa*. For instance, @barley_1959 reported defecation rates of approximately 0.32 g dry faeces g${-1}\$ fresh weight d$^{-1}$ in small individuals and 0.22 g dry faeces g$^{-1}$ fresh weight d$^{-1}$ in larger individuals. Interpreted literally, the estimated $[\dot{F}_m]$ would therefore imply unrealistically high soil ingestion rates. This discrepancy suggests that $[\dot{F}_m]$ should not be interpreted as a direct measure of ingestion capacity, but rather as an effective parameter compensating for simplified representation of food acquisition and assimilation. In the current formulation, ingestion is assumed to depend on organic matter density in the cosm, but earthworms may selectively ingest organic-rich microsites, mucus-enriched soil, microbial biomass, or partially decomposed dung particles rather than processing soil homogenously. In addition, the energetic value of horse dung was fixed, although its actual assimilable energy may vary with decomposition state and microbial activity.

Compared with @gergs_2022, the present model produced parameter estimates that appear more consistent with earthworm physiology and the amount of energy available in the environment from soil organic matter and horse dung. In particular, the lower estimates of $\{\dot p_{Am}\}$ and $[\dot F_m]$ reduced the unrealistically high energy inputs implied by the previous formulation, while estimated structural density ($d_V = 1.2 \mathrm{g.cm}^{-3}$) was more compatible with earthworm tissue density than the value of 0.27 $\mathrm{g.cm}^{-3}$ reported in @gergs_2022. Their parametrization also implies a much higher initial egg reserve of 131 J per cocoon, compared with about 68.4 J in the present model. Together, these differences suggest that the formulation of @gergs_2022 relies on substantially larger energy fluxes than those supported by available physiological and environmental information.

Parameter compensation and identifiability issues are common in DEB model calibration, particularly for ingestion- and assimilation-related parameters that integrate multiple physiological processes [@lika_2011]. In the present model, this issue is particularly relevant for the parameters governing energy acquisition. The digestion efficiency ($\kappa_X$) was fixed based on nitrogen assimilation efficiencies reported by @whalen_1999, but this value remains uncertain and may vary with the type and quality of ingested organic matter. Moreover, $\kappa_X$ is mathematically closely linked to the maximum searching rate ($[\dot{F}_m]$), since both parameters determine the amount of energy ultimately assimilated from ingested material. As a result, these parameters may partly compensate each other during calibration.

Consistent with this interpretation, a strong posterior correlation was observed between $[\dot{F}_m]$ and $K$ (@fig-corr_D_CWKe), indicating that the maximum searching rate and the curvature of the functional response also partly compensate each other. The condition-specific biases observed across feeding treatments further indicate that the relationship between organic matter availability and assimilated energy remains imperfectly constrained. In particular, deviations were not strictly proportional to the amount of food supplied, suggesting that food accessibility, selection, and digestive efficiency may be as important as food quantity itself.

Together, these results suggest that $[\dot{F}_m]$, $\kappa_X$, and $K$ should be interpreted cautiously as effective parameters describing overall energy acquisition rather than as independent direct measurements of ingestion, digestion, or assimilation processes. This highlights the need for additional experimental data on ingestion, egestion, gut transit time, organic matter selection, and digestion efficiency to better constrain the mechanistic link between environmental organic matter and assimilated energy. The sensitivity analysis further supported this interpretation. While individual-level endpoints were only weakly sensitive to most ingestion-related parameters under the selected scenario, the amount of organic matter remaining in the cosm was influenced by a broader set of parameters, particularly $[\dot{F}_m]$, $\kappa_X$, $\mu_{OM}$, and $d_V$. This indicates that environmental resource dynamics integrate several interacting processes related to ingestion, digestion, organic matter transformation, and individual growth. It also confirms that these processes are difficult to disentangle using body-mass and reproduction data alone.

The present results also suggest that reserve contribution to fresh body mass is relatively very limited in *A. caliginosa*. The formulation assuming reserve mass independent of structural size consistently outperformed alternatives where reserve contribution scaled proportionally with structure. Similar formulations have been used in other DEB applications to better represent body mass dynamics under variable feeding conditions [@dortel_2020]. In addition, experiment Bart2019_XP3 showed little decrease in fresh body mass during periods where reserves were expected to decline, suggesting that reserve depletion contributes only weakly to total wet mass under the tested conditions.

## Energy allocation and the $\kappa$ rule

The model comparison strongly supported formulations including a progressive transition in energy allocation after maturation. Models assuming a fixed allocation rule throughout the life cycle systematically produced poorer fits and resulted in unrealistic estimates for the energy content of cocoons. In contrast, introducing a gradual transition in allocation over a characteristic timescale $\tau$ substantially improved model performance.

These results provide an intermediate interpretation between the standard DEB $\kappa$-rule and alternative energy-budget formulations proposed for earthworms. @johnston_2014 argued that reproduction should take priority over growth in adults and therefore rejected the standard DEB assumption of a constant allocation fraction. In contrast, @rakel_2020 showed that the standard DEB model could adequately reproduce several experimental datasets when density effects were interpreted as competition for food and crowding rather than as changes in allocation strategy. More recently, @gergs_2022 reproduced the growth plateau observed when adults were grouped by introducing different functional response values between juvenile and grouped-adult phases, effectively modifying energy assimilation after grouping.

Our results suggest that neither a strictly constant nor an instantaneous allocation shift fully captures the observed dynamics. The progressive transition introduced here allowed the model to better reproduce the smoother growth trajectories observed in the new density experiments, where juveniles were maintained in groups from the beginning of the experiment. In contrast, experiments from @bart_2019 displayed a sharper transition between juvenile growth and adult plateau, likely because adults were grouped only after maturity had been detected. Importantly, in these experiments, individuals experience a transient period during which they are physiologically mature but still isolated, because pairing occurs only after the experimenter identifies adulthood. During this interval, worms are effectively in an “isolated adult” state, with reproductive opportunities still absent. In the present model, this situation is represented by maintaining the juvenile allocation fraction ($\kappa_J$) until grouping occurs, after which a progressive transition toward the adult allocation fraction ($\kappa_A$) is initiated.

More generally, these results are consistent with the view that energy allocation may shift during ontogeny as organisms transition from growth-dominated juvenile stages to reproductive adult stages. Such stage-dependent allocation patterns have been documented in other organisms, for instance in *Daphnia magna*, where age affects the balance between somatic growth and reproductive investment [@glazier_1992]. These shifts also fit within the broader framework of life-history trade-offs, in which organisms allocate limited resources among competing functions such as growth, maintenance, and reproduction [@zera_2001]. In *A. caliginosa*, the progressive transition in $\kappa$ may therefore reflect both physiological changes associated with maturation and delays associated with the establishment of reproductive interactions.

The sensitivity analysis further showed that the representation of energy allocation is a major determinant of model behaviour. When temperature-related parameters were excluded, ($\kappa_J$) was the main driver of body mass, maturity, and reproduction at both juvenile and adult stages. Its influence across several endpoints indicates that uncertainty in juvenile allocation propagates throughout the life cycle, including after the transition toward the adult allocation strategy. This result supports the importance of accurately constraining allocation parameters, while also showing that several life-history predictions depend on a common underlying allocation mechanism.

Simulations of isolated adults nevertheless highlighted limitations in the current representation of post-maturation energy allocation. The growth trajectories observed for isolated adults suggest that these individuals occupy an intermediate physiological state between juveniles and grouped reproducing adults. Maintaining the juvenile allocation strategy throughout adulthood resulted in unrealistic continuous growth, whereas reallocating energy toward reproduction as in grouped adults produced growth plateaus that were too pronounced. These results indicate that isolated adults cannot be fully described either as juveniles or as reproductively active adults within the current DEB formulations. Additionally, this formulation still predicted a non-zero reproductive flux after maturation despite the absence of observed cocoon production.

This discrepancy raises the question of the energetic fate of reproduction-related fluxes in isolated individuals. One possible interpretation is that isolated adults still undergo physiological changes associated with maturation, including maturity maintenance costs, but that the energy normally allocated to reproduction is partly redirected toward somatic growth or reserve dynamics in the absence of mating opportunities. Such a mechanism could explain why isolated adults continue growing after maturation. More generally, these results suggest that isolated adults represent a distinct physiological state that is not fully captured by the current DEB formulations.

However, the ecological relevance of isolated adults remains limited. Under natural conditions, complete reproductive isolation is likely rare except at very low population densities. Consequently, the isolated-adult experiments should primarily be viewed as a useful conceptual framework for exploring energy allocation dynamics rather than as a realistic representation of field conditions.

## Model limitations and perspectives

Despite generally good agreement between simulations and observations, some systematic discrepancies remained. Growth plateaus were under or overestimated in some grouped adult experiments, and the transition between juvenile and adult growth remained more abrupt in several density treatments than in observations. The predicted-observed analyses further indicated that fit quality decreased under high juvenile densities and low per-capita food availability, with negative residuals suggesting an underestimation of juvenile body mass under the most limiting conditions (Figure 4). These patterns indicate that deviations were not randomly distributed across datasets, but were associated with specific combinations of density, food availability, developmental stage, and experimental context.

The model also did not reproduce the reduction in reproductive output observed for grouped individuals under low-food conditions (Bart2019_XP4_2) when density increased from three to six individuals per cosm under non-ad libitum feeding conditions. However, this effect was observed in only a single experimental condition, preventing robust incorporation of an explicit density-dependent reproductive process into the model. Reproductive output was also overestimated for individuals at density 2 with high food conditions (Gollot2026_D2b), where observed reproduction remained lower than predicted despite high food availability. This suggests that increasing food availability does not necessarily translate into proportional increases in reproduction and that additional processes, such as reproductive timing, individual variability, or microenvironmental effects, may influence cocoon production. As discussed above, the underestimation of juvenile growth at high population densities suggests that the assumption of homogeneous resource sharing may be too simplistic.

Overall, these discrepancies likely reflect processes that are not explicitly represented in the current formulation. First, effects of density may not be exclusively mediated by resource limitation. Behavioural interactions, spatial interference, or asymmetric competition may influence ingestion and reproduction. Second, variability among individuals in maturation timing may smooth the population-level transition between juvenile and adult stages, whereas the current model assumes homogeneous transition dynamics. Third, the quantification of ingestion and assimilation processes in endogeic earthworms remains highly uncertain because individuals feed directly within the soil matrix. Consequently, the ingestion-related parameters likely integrate multiple processes, including soil exploration, ingestion, digestion, gut transit, and assimilation efficiency. The sensitivity analysis also highlighted the strong influence of temperature correction on model predictions. When thermal parameters were included, $T_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $T_H$ dominated the sensitivity of most endpoints, and total-order indices were frequently much larger than first-order indices. This indicates substantial interactions between temperature correction, energy allocation, and other physiological processes. Accurate characterization of thermal dependence is therefore essential before applying the model across variable temperature regimes. However, because $T_{\mathrm{ref}}$ acts as a reference value in the correction of all physiological rates, its dominant sensitivity partly reflects its structural role in the model rather than the influence of a single biological process.

Future work should therefore focus on improving the mechanistic representation of energy acquisition and the thermal dependence of physiological rates in earthworms. In particular, experiments manipulating both the quantity and energetic quality of organic matter sources would help disentangle the respective roles of food quantity, food quality, and resource accessibility. Characterizing the energetic value of different organic amendments and soil organic matter fractions could further improve the mechanistic representation of assimilation processes and the estimation of functional response parameters. Simultaneous monitoring of organic matter depletion, ingestion, defecation, and cocoon production would also provide valuable constraints for assimilation-related parameters and the functional response.

## Conclusion

This study developed a Dynamic Energy Budget model for *Aporrectodea caliginosa* integrating the effects of food availability, organic matter quality, and population density on growth, maturation, and reproduction. By combining literature datasets with new density experiments performed from the juvenile stage onward, the model was calibrated across a broader range of life stages and environmental conditions than previous earthworm DEB studies.

The results indicate that reduced juvenile growth at higher densities can largely be explained by lower per-capita resource availability and food acquisition, without requiring an explicit density-dependent effect on energy assimilation. In contrast, the best-performing formulations also showed that post-maturation energy allocation cannot be adequately described by a strictly constant allocation rule throughout the life cycle. Introducing a progressive transition in energy allocation after maturation substantially improved the description of adult growth and reproduction trajectories, suggesting that reproductive opportunities shape energetic allocation in adult earthworms.

A major contribution of this work is the mechanistic linkage established between the functional response and environmental organic matter availability. The model explicitly distinguishes the energetic contribution of horse dung and soil organic matter, highlighting the limited assimilable energetic value of native soil organic matter relative to fresh organic amendments. The study also emphasizes the methodological difficulties associated with quantifying ingestion and assimilation processes in endogeic earthworms, which remain important limitations for mechanistic modelling approaches.

Overall, the proposed framework improves the ecological realism of DEB modelling for soil invertebrates by strengthening the links between environmental variability and individual energetics. This work provides a robust basis for future developments integrating multiple stressors, including pesticides and climate-related constraints, within DEB-TKTD modelling approaches for earthworms.
