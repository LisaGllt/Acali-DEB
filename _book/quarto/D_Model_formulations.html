5.1 Classication DEB standard formulation

Reserve energy dynamic

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm d t} = \frac{\{\dot p_{Am}\}_t}{L} \ \left(f - \frac{E\ \nu_t}{\{\dot p_{Am}\}_t}\right) \]

With :

  • \(E\) : Energy in the reserve (\(J\))
  • \(\nu_t\) : Energy conductance at temperature \(T_{exp}\) (\(cm.d^{-1}\))

Growth

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm d L}{\mathrm d t} &= \frac{\nu_t}{3\ (\frac{E}{E_m} + g)} \left(\frac{E}{E_m} - \frac{L}{L_m}\right) \\ \\ K_m &= \frac{[\dot p_{M,t}]}{[E_G]} \\ E_m &= \frac{\{\dot p_{Am}\}_t}{\nu_t}\\ g &= \frac{[E_G]}{\kappa_{\text{real}}\ E_m}\\ L_m &= \frac{\nu_t}{K_m\ g} \end{aligned} \]

With :

  • \(L\) : Structural volumetric length (\(cm\))
  • \([\dot p_{M,t}]\) : Volume-specific maintenance rate (\(J.d^{-1}.cm^{-3}\))
  • \([E_G]\) : Volume-specific cost for structure (\(J.cm^{-3}\))

\[ W_W = L^3 \left(1+\frac{E}{E_m}w\right) \]

With :

  • \(W_W\) : Wet weight of the earthworm (\(g\))
  • \(w\) : Contribution of reserve to body weight (\(g.cm^{-3}\))

Maturity and reproduction

\[ \dot p_C = \frac{g\ E}{g + \frac{E}{E_m}} (\nu_t\ L^2 + K_m\ L^3) \]

With :

  • \(\dot p_C\) : Mobilized energy flux from reserve (\(J.d^{-1}\))

\[ \dot p_R = (1-\kappa_{\text{real}})\ \dot p_C - \dot k_{J,t}\ E_h \]

With :

  • \(\dot p_R\) : Allocated energy flux to reproduction (\(J.d^{-1}\))
  • \(\dot k_{J,t}\) : Maturity maintenance rate (\(d^{-1}\))

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}E_H}{\mathrm{d}t}= \begin{cases} E_H \leq E_H^p,\ & \dot{p}_R\\ E_H > E_H^p,\ & 0 \end{cases} \]

With :

  • \(E_h\) : Maturity (\(J\))
  • \(E_h^p\) : Maturity at puberty (\(J\))

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t}= \begin{cases} E_H \leq E_H^p,\ & 0 \\ E_H > E_H^p,\ & \kappa_R\ \frac{\dot{p}_R}{E_{0}} \end{cases} \]

With :

  • \(R\) : Cumulated reproduction (#)
  • \(\kappa_R\) : Reproduction efficiency (-)
  • \(E_0\) : Energy cost for an egg (\(J\))

Effect of temperature on rates

\[ \begin{aligned} s_A &= \exp(\frac{T_A}{T_{ref}-\frac{T_A}{T_{exp}+273.15}})\\ s_{rH} &= \frac{1 + \exp (\frac{T_A^H}{T^H}-\frac{T_A^H}{T_{ref}+ 273.15})}{1 + \exp (\frac{T_A^H}{T^H}-\frac{T_A^H}{T_{exp}+273.15})}\\ T_c &= s_A (T_{exp}+273.15 \geq T_{ref}) s_{rH} + (T_{exp} + 273.15 < T_{ref})\\ \\ \{\dot p_{Am}\}_t &= \{\dot p_{Am}\}\ T_c\\ [\dot p_{M,t}] &= [\dot p_{M}]\ T_c \\ \nu_t &= \nu \ T_c \\ \dot k_{J,t} &= \dot k_{J} \ T_c \end{aligned} \]

5.2 Tested model formulations

Our model formulation is based on the standard DEB model (Kooijman, 2009), using the extended model developed by Gergs et al. (2022) as a starting point. Model notation and terminology follow the conventions defined in standard DEB theory (Kooijman, 2009). The standard DEB model describes four primary state variables at the individual level of the earthworm : (i) Structural length \(L\); (ii) Energy of the reserve, \(E\); (iii) Maturity, \(E_h\); and (iv) Reproduction, \(R\), corresponding to the cumulated number of offspring produced by the individual. Environmental state variables are also taken into account as inputs (temperature, organic matter content). Model parameters are detailed in Table tbl-chap3Param, while state and intermediate variables are listed in Table tbl-art4ModStateVar.

Several alternative model formulations were evaluated to test key biological assumptions.

Our primary objective was to mechanistically link the quantity and quality of available organic matter to the functional response (\(f\)). Organic matter from soil and horse dung were treated separately due to differences in quality and bioavailability. Horse dung was assumed to contain 90% organic matter with an energy density of 3100 kcal/kg (13 000 J/g, \(\mu_{OM}\)) (Chastain, 2022; Sahu et al., 2016). In contrast, soil organic matter was assumed to provide less usable energy. To account for this difference, a scaling factor (\(r_{OM}\)) was introduced to relate the energy content of soil organic matter to that of horse dung.

The ingestion rate was formulated as a function of the maximum surface-area-specific searching rate, \(\{\dot{F}_m\}\), or the maximum mass-specific searching rate, \([\dot{F}_m]\), individual size, organic matter availability, and population density (Equation eq-chap3Assim). Specifically, the ingested energy depends on structural size or structural mass, the quantities of soil and horse organic matter in the cosm (\(OM{soil}\), \(OM_{horse}\) in \(\mathrm{g_{OM}}\)), their respective energetic contributions, and the number of individuals (\(Nb_{ind}\)).

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot Q_f^{matter} &= \{\dot{F}_m\}\ L^2 & (\text{version L}) \\ \dot Q_f^{matter} &= [\dot{F}_m]\ d_V L^3 & (\text{version W}) \\ \\ \dot Q_{f}^{soil} &= \dot Q_f^{matter} \times \frac{OM_{soil}}{Soil_{w,cosm}\ Nb_{ind}} \times \mu_{OM}\ r_{OM}\\ \dot Q_{f}^{horse} &= \dot Q_f^{matter} \times \frac{OM_{horse}}{Soil_{w,cosm}\ Nb_{ind}} \times \mu_{OM} \\ \\ \dot Q_{f,tot} &= \dot Q_{f,horse} + \dot Q_{f,soil} \end{aligned} \tag{5.1}\]

Consumption by individuals was explicitly incorporated into the temporal dynamics of organic matter in the cosm with the calculation of the rates of consumption of horse dung and soil organic matter (\(\dot{OM}_{out}^{horse}\) and \(\dot{OM}_{out}^{soil}\) in \(\mathrm{g_{OM}.d^{-1}}\)) (Equation eq-chap3OMdyn).

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm d OM_{soil}}{\mathrm d t} &= -\frac{\dot Q_{f}^{soil}}{\mu_{OM}\ r_{OM}}\ Nb_{ind}\\ \frac{\mathrm d OM_{horse}}{\mathrm d t} &= -\frac{\dot Q_{f}^{horse}}{\mu_{OM}}\ Nb_{ind} \end{aligned} \tag{5.2}\]

The functional response (\(f\)) was derived as a saturating function of the ratio between ingested energy and maximal ingestion capacity. Within the DEB framework, \(f\) represents the degree to which food availability limits assimilation, ranging from food limitation (\(f \approx 0\)) to ad libitum conditions (\(f \approx 1\)). A saturating relationship was therefore used to reflect the progressive increase in assimilated energy as ingestion approaches the maximal capacity of the organism. Two alternative formulations were tested (Equation eq-chap3funres): (i) a classical hyperbolic response, commonly used in DEB applications, in which \(f = 0.5\) when ingested energy reaches half of its maximum value, and (ii) a normalized hyperbolic formulation constraining \(f\) between 0 and 1 such that \(f = 1\) when ingestion reaches its maximal value. This formulation allows maximal ingestion to correspond biologically to ad libitum feeding conditions, where additional food no longer increases assimilation and life-history performance.

\[ \begin{aligned} f &= \frac{X}{1+X} &\text{with }\ X &= \frac{\dot Q_{f,tot}}{\frac 1 2 \dot Q_{f,max}} &\text{(version Y)} \\ f &= \frac{X}{K\ (1-X)+X} &\text{with }\ X &= \frac{\dot Q_{f,tot}}{\dot Q_{f,max}} &\text{(version K)} \end{aligned} \tag{5.3}\]

Reserve mobilization, growth, maturation, and reproduction follow standard DEB equations with temperature correction. A baseline formulation assuming a fixed allocation fraction throughout the life cycle was considered (equation version F), consistent with the standard DEB \(\kappa\)-rule. In addition, we introduced a model variant with a context-dependent allocation rule for \(\kappa\) to account for reproductive opportunities (equation version C, Equation eq-chap3kappaC). Specifically, energy allocation shifts when individuals reach maturity (at time \(t_p\)), reflecting the biological constraint that reproduction requires the presence of conspecifics. This transition can occur progressively over a characteristic timescale \(\tau\).

\[ \begin{aligned} x &= \begin{cases} \text{if } E_h < E_h^p, & 0 \\ \text{if } E_h \geq E_h^p, & \min \left( 1, \frac{t-t_p}{\tau}\right) \end{cases} \\ \kappa_{real} &= (1-x)\kappa_J + x\ \kappa_A \end{aligned} \tag{5.4}\]

Finally, two alternative formulations were considered to relate structural size (\(L\)) to fresh body mass (\(W_W\)), reflecting different assumptions regarding reserve (\(E\)) contribution to total body weight. In the first formulation, reserve contribution was assumed proportional to structural size, consistent with the weak homeostasis assumption of DEB theory, whereby reserve covaries with structure. In the second formulation, reserve contribution was represented independently of structural size, allowing fresh body mass to vary according to reserve dynamics independently of structural growth (Equation eq-chap3RLW).

\[ \begin{aligned} W_W &= L^3 \left( d_V + \frac{E}{E_m} w_E \right) & \text{(version w)} \\ W_W &= L^3 \ d_V + E\ d_E & \text{(version e)} \end{aligned} \tag{5.5}\]

with \(d_V\) the density of structure (g.cm\(^{-3}\)), \(d_E\) the density of reserve (g.J\(^{-1}\)), \(w_E\) the Contribution of reserve to body weight (g.cm\(^{-3}\)) and \(E_m\) the maximum reserve (J).

Models are designated with a combination of letters corresponding to the different variants of equations.

5.3 Model parametrization and calibration

The DEB model was calibrated and evaluated using both literature datasets and the new density experiment conducted in this study. Together, these datasets cover multiple life stages and experimental conditions, including isolated individuals, grouped adults, and juveniles reared under different density and food availability conditions. The main characteristics of each dataset, including density treatment and feeding conditions are summarized in Table tbl-chap3XP (more detail sec-DataSynth). This combination of datasets provides complementary information for characterizing growth, maturation, and reproduction processes across a range of environmental conditions (temperature, feeding, population density).

Model calibration was performed exclusively using datasets corresponding to juvenile individuals and adults maintained in groups. Data from isolated adults were excluded from the calibration procedure because reproduction cannot occur in the absence of mating partners, potentially affecting energy allocation patterns and biasing the estimation of reproductive parameters.

Model parameters were estimated within a Bayesian framework using largely weakly informative priors, while incorporating more constrained priors for parameters with available biological or physiological knowledge. The full set of prior distributions is detailed in sec-DEB. The models were implemented in the MCSim environment. Parameter inference was performed using Bayesian numerical calibration following Bois (2009). Likelihood functions were defined assuming normally distributed errors for body weight and Poisson-distributed counts for reproduction. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations were run with 3 chains for 100,000 iterations to approximate posterior distributions of model parameters which were estimated from the last 10,000 iterations of the MCMC chains. The point estimates correspond to the posterior distributions mode.

Model comparison was based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which balances goodness-of-fit and model complexity by penalizing models with a higher number of parameters, but also on the RMSE of each experiment. In addition, to investigate whether model performance and prediction bias varied across experimental conditions, predicted-observed relationships were examined according to temperature, density, food availability, and developmental stage.

A global sensitivity analysis was conducted on the best-performing model using the Sobol method with the Jansen estimator, implemented in the “sensitivity” R package (Ioss et al., 2026). Two complementary analyses were performed: one including the parameters governing temperature dependence and one excluding them, in order to distinguish the contribution of thermal correction from that of the remaining physiological and environmental parameters. Parameter values were varied independently within ±10% of their posterior estimates to quantify their relative influence on model outputs.

The sensitivity analysis was based on simulations of the Gollot2026_D2 experiment, in which two earthworms were maintained per cosm under moderate food availability. Body mass, reserve energy, and maturity were evaluated at both the juvenile stage (\(t = 50\) days) and the adult stage (\(t = 140\) days). Reproductive output at \(t = 140\) days and the total amount of organic matter remaining in the cosm at \(t = 50\) days were also included as endpoints. These endpoints were selected to characterize parameter influence on energy acquisition, individual state, life-history transitions, and reproductive performance at contrasting developmental stages.

Finally, the case of isolated adults was therefore explored only through simulations to investigate the resulting patterns of energy allocation in the absence of reproductive opportunities. Two alternative formulations were considered: (i) maintaining the juvenile allocation fraction throughout adulthood (\(\kappa_J\)), and (ii) introducing a transition toward the adult allocation fraction (\(\kappa_A\)) after maturation, as implemented for grouped adults.

## Model parametrization and calibration

The DEB model was calibrated and evaluated using both literature datasets and the new density experiment conducted in this study. Together, these datasets cover multiple life stages and experimental conditions, including isolated individuals, grouped adults, and juveniles reared under different density and food availability conditions. The main characteristics of each dataset, including density treatment and feeding conditions are summarized in @tbl-chap3XP (more detail @sec-DataSynth). This combination of datasets provides complementary information for characterizing growth, maturation, and reproduction processes across a range of environmental conditions (temperature, feeding, population density).

Model calibration was performed exclusively using datasets corresponding to juvenile individuals and adults maintained in groups. Data from isolated adults were excluded from the calibration procedure because reproduction cannot occur in the absence of mating partners, potentially affecting energy allocation patterns and biasing the estimation of reproductive parameters.

Model parameters were estimated within a Bayesian framework using largely weakly informative priors, while incorporating more constrained priors for parameters with available biological or physiological knowledge. The full set of prior distributions is detailed in @sec-DEB. The models were implemented in the MCSim environment. Parameter inference was performed using Bayesian numerical calibration following @bois_2009. Likelihood functions were defined assuming normally distributed errors for body weight and Poisson-distributed counts for reproduction. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations were run with 3 chains for 100,000 iterations to approximate posterior distributions of model parameters which were estimated from the last 10,000 iterations of the MCMC chains. The point estimates correspond to the posterior distributions mode.

Model comparison was based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which balances goodness-of-fit and model complexity by penalizing models with a higher number of parameters, but also on the RMSE of each experiment. In addition, to investigate whether model performance and prediction bias varied across experimental conditions, predicted-observed relationships were examined according to temperature, density, food availability, and developmental stage.

A global sensitivity analysis was conducted on the best-performing model using the Sobol method with the Jansen estimator, implemented in the “sensitivity” R package [@ioss_2026]. Two complementary analyses were performed: one including the parameters governing temperature dependence and one excluding them, in order to distinguish the contribution of thermal correction from that of the remaining physiological and environmental parameters. Parameter values were varied independently within ±10% of their posterior estimates to quantify their relative influence on model outputs.

The sensitivity analysis was based on simulations of the Gollot2026_D2 experiment, in which two earthworms were maintained per cosm under moderate food availability. Body mass, reserve energy, and maturity were evaluated at both the juvenile stage ($t = 50$ days) and the adult stage ($t = 140$ days). Reproductive output at $t = 140$ days and the total amount of organic matter remaining in the cosm at $t = 50$ days were also included as endpoints. These endpoints were selected to characterize parameter influence on energy acquisition, individual state, life-history transitions, and reproductive performance at contrasting developmental stages.

Finally, the case of isolated adults was therefore explored only through simulations to investigate the resulting patterns of energy allocation in the absence of reproductive opportunities. Two alternative formulations were considered: (i) maintaining the juvenile allocation fraction throughout adulthood ($\kappa_J$), and (ii) introducing a transition toward the adult allocation fraction ($\kappa_A$) after maturation, as implemented for grouped adults.
