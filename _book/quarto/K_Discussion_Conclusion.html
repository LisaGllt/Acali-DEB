12  Discussion & Conclusions

12.1 Single compound effect

The DEB-TKTD analysis confirmed the marked difference in toxicity between imidacloprid and epoxiconazole observed in Aporrectodea caliginosa, for which reproduction EC\(_{50}\) values were previously estimated at 0.55 and 126.8 mg\(\cdot\)kg\(^{-1}\) for imidacloprid and epoxiconazole, respectively (Gollot et al., 2026). The internal no-effect concentrations was estimated at 1,122 [893 ; 1,511] ng\(\cdot\)g\(^{-1}\) for imidacloprid, compared with 47,992 [15,105; 100,090] ng\(\cdot\)g\(^{-1}\) for epoxiconazole, indicating that physiological stress was initiated at a substantially lower internal exposure for imidacloprid. This difference is consistent with the literature with reported reproduction EC\(_{50}\) values of 0.21-1.89 mg\(\cdot\)kg\(^{-1}\) in Eisenia species, depending on soil properties and experimental conditions (Bandeira et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019).

At toxicokinetic equilibrium, the internal no-effect concentrations can be translated into approximate external soil concentrations using the bioaccumulation factors previously estimated for the two compounds. Based on a bioaccumulation factor of 21, the imidacloprid no-effect concentration corresponds to an external concentration of approximately 0.053 [0.041 ; 0.072] mg\(\cdot\)kg\(^{-1}\). For epoxiconazole, using a bioaccumulation factor of 0.9, the corresponding external concentration is approximately 53.3 [16.8 ; 111.2] mg\(\cdot\)kg\(^{-1}\). These external-equivalent thresholds are consistent with the concentration ranges over which effects were observed experimentally and further illustrate the much greater potency of imidacloprid. However, these conversions should be interpreted cautiously because they assume constant exposure and toxicokinetic equilibrium.

12.2 Physiological modes of action

The physiological modes of action selected for the two pesticides both involved a reduction in assimilation, although the evidence for the additional affected process differed between compounds. For imidacloprid and epoxiconazole, the best-supported models combined effects on assimilation with growth and reproduction, respectively (Table 11.1 & Table 10.1). However, the differences among the best-performing epoxiconazole models were small, and formulations combining growth and reproduction or assimilation and growth received nearly equivalent support. Reduced assimilation therefore emerged as a plausible component of the response to both pesticides, but the available data did not allow the physiological effects of epoxiconazole to be attributed unambiguously to a specific combination of energetic processes.

The biological interpretation of these functional pMoAs is nevertheless consistent with known effects of the two compounds. Imidacloprid is a neuroactive pesticide that can alter activity, feeding behavior, and burrowing in earthworms, while also affecting detoxification pathways and cellular integrity (Dittbrenner et al., 2010; Kaur et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2019; Zang et al., 2000). Reduced feeding or food processing could therefore appear in the DEB model as impaired assimilation, whereas effects on growth may summarize increased energetic costs of tissue production, detoxification, or cellular repair.

Epoxiconazole exposure has been associated with oxidative stress, depletion of energetic reserves, and reduced reproductive performance in earthworms (Bart et al., 2019, 2020; Givaudan, 2014; Pelosi et al., 2016). In addition, epoxiconazole is recognized as a reproductive toxicant in mammals, with reported effects on steroid hormone levels and reproductive development (Stinchcombe et al., 2013; Taxvig et al., 2007). Although these mechanisms cannot be directly extrapolated to earthworms, they support the biological plausibility of an additional effect on reproduction.

Within the present DEB-TKTD framework, such mechanisms are represented through changes in energetic fluxes rather than modeled explicitly. The selected pMoAs should therefore be interpreted as functional descriptions of the processes required to reproduce the observed life-history responses, rather than as direct evidence for specific biochemical mechanisms.

Accurate representation of the control responses was a prerequisite for estimating pesticide effects within the DEB-TKTD framework. Any systematic discrepancy between the baseline DEB predictions and the control observations could otherwise be incorrectly attributed to toxic stress, thereby biasing the estimates of the no-effect concentration, stress slope, and physiological mode of action. In the present study, the original DEB parameterization slightly underestimated juvenile and adult growth while overestimating cocoon production. Re-estimating the maximum surface-area-specific assimilation rate, \(\{\dot{p}_{Am}\}\), the cocoon energy content, \(E_{coc}\), and the adult allocation fraction, \(\kappa_A\), from the control data improved the representation of baseline growth and reproduction before fitting the toxicodynamic parameters. This step was particularly important because systematic discrepancies in untreated organisms could otherwise have been incorrectly attributed to toxic stress, thereby biasing the estimated no-effect concentration and stress slope or artificially increasing support for particular physiological modes of action. Although the recalibrated model substantially reduced the discrepancy in reproduction, cocoon production remained slightly overestimated. Consequently, part of the support for a direct reproductive pMoA for epoxiconazole may reflect this residual mismatch in the baseline model rather than a compound-specific effect alone. The interpretation of reproductive pMoAs should therefore remain cautious. More generally, these results emphasize that DEB-TKTD calibration should first ensure an adequate representation of control organisms before identifying compound-specific toxicodynamic processes.

12.3 Model limitations and perspective of improvement

The selected models generally captured the concentration-dependent responses in juvenile growth, adult growth, and reproduction, although some systematic deviations remained for both compounds. For epoxiconazole, the combined assimilation and reproduction model reproduced the overall response patterns but underestimated growth effects at the lowest tested concentrations and reproductive effects at the highest concentrations. These discrepancies remained relatively limited, but they indicate that the selected pMoA did not fully capture the shape of the response across the entire exposure gradient. For imidacloprid, the model underestimated juvenile growth and reproduction at intermediate concentrations, while cocoon production was slightly overestimated at the highest concentrations.

Several processes may contribute to these residual patterns. First, the threshold-linear stress function may be too restrictive to describe a nonlinear or relatively abrupt transition between low and pronounced effects. Alternative formulations, such as a Hill-type stress function, could provide greater flexibility in the shape of the toxicodynamic response. Second, imidacloprid toxicokinetics showed evidence of saturation at high exposure levels, restricting the application domain of the TK model. Internal concentrations, and consequently toxic effects, may therefore be less accurately predicted for the highest treatments. Finally, adult mortality observed at the highest imidacloprid concentration could not be reproduced because survival was not explicitly represented in the present model. Predictions at this concentration therefore describe only sublethal growth and reproduction processes and do not account for the loss of individuals during exposure.

Although the estimated no-effect concentrations provide a mechanistically relevant measure of sensitivity, their credible intervals remained relatively broad, particularly for epoxiconazole. This uncertainty likely results partly from the distribution of tested concentrations. Relatively few treatments were located close to the inferred onset of effects, whereas many observations corresponded either to concentrations producing little response or to concentrations already associated with marked toxicity. Additional experimental treatments concentrated around the estimated no-effect concentrations would improve identification of the threshold and help distinguish more clearly between competing pMoAs.

The available experimental data also covered separate and very restricted portions of the life cycle rather than following individuals continuously from hatching to adulthood and reproduction. Endpoints were measured over a short period, with juvenile growth assessed at only three time points, adult growth at two time points, and cocoon production at a single time point over 28 days. This limited temporal resolution makes it difficult to distinguish among physiological modes of action, as several pMoAs may produce similar responses over short exposure periods but differ in their longer-term effects on ultimate size, maturation timing, or reproductive rate (Jager, 2015). Combining these endpoints within a single DEB-TKTD framework provides a more integrated analysis than endpoint-specific dose-response models, but it does not fully constrain the temporal propagation of toxic effects across the life cycle. Experiments following individuals from emergence to maturity and reproduction under continuous exposure would provide stronger information on delayed maturation, carry-over effects, changes in energy allocation between life stages, and the discrimination among competing pMoAs.

Finally, effects on embryonic development could not be incorporated into the present model because the available data were insufficient to estimate an embryo-specific damage process. Although cocoon production was quantified, the model did not explicitly represent potential effects on cocoon quality, embryonic development time, hatching success, or hatchling condition. This limitation may be important because reproductive toxicity can arise not only through reduced maternal investment but also through direct exposure of cocoons and embryos. Future DEB-TKTD developments should therefore integrate embryonic development and hatching data, ideally within experiments covering the complete life cycle. Such data would allow discrimination between effects on adult energy allocation, cocoon production, and embryo viability, thereby improving both the mechanistic interpretation and predictive capacity of the model.

12.4 Towards a DEB-TKTD for the mixture

A first step toward a DEB-TKTD model for the mixture would be to predict combined effects directly from the independently calibrated single-compound models, without introducing an additional interaction parameter. This approach would provide a mechanistic reference prediction against which the observed mixture responses could be compared and would allow assessment of whether accounting explicitly for the toxicokinetics and physiological effects of each compound improves mixture predictions relative to conventional external concentration-based approaches.

Because no toxicokinetic interaction was detected between imidacloprid and epoxiconazole, their internal concentration dynamics can initially be simulated using the single-compound TK models. The resulting compound-specific internal concentrations would then generate separate damage and stress variables, which could be combined at the level of the affected DEB processes. Although both compounds were associated with reduced assimilation in the selected DEB-TKTD formulations, this common pMoA does not necessarily imply that they act through the same molecular or cellular target. A DEB pMoA represents an aggregated effect on an energetic flux and may encompass several distinct biochemical, behavioral, or physiological mechanisms. Imidacloprid and epoxiconazole could therefore still be considered independent at the underlying mechanism level, given their different primary modes of action.

Under an Independent Action assumption, the compound-specific stress modifiers can be combined multiplicatively. Thus, a DEB process modified by a factor \((1 \pm s)\) for a single compound would be modified by \((1 \pm s_{\mathrm{IMD}})(1 \pm s_{\mathrm{EPX}})\) under joint exposure (Jager et al., 2010; Matyja, 2023). The sign depends on whether stress increases or decreases the affected process.

This formulation preserves the separate TK and TD dynamics of each pesticide while generating a prediction of their combined effect in the absence of an explicit interaction. Comparing these predictions with the observed mixture data would help determine whether the apparent mixture response can be explained by independent physiological effects acting simultaneously or whether an additional interaction term is required.

The reliability of this approach nevertheless depends on the predictive performance of the single-compound models. The epoxiconazole model reproduced juvenile growth, adult growth, and reproduction without a strong systematic residual pattern, apart from a slight underestimation of growth effects at the highest concentrations. Its contribution to mixture predictions is therefore expected to be reasonably well characterized. In contrast, the imidacloprid model overestimated effects at intermediate concentrations and underestimated them at the highest concentrations. These systematic deviations may propagate into mixture predictions, leading to excessive predicted effects when the imidacloprid contribution is intermediate and insufficient predicted effects in mixtures dominated by high imidacloprid exposure. Consequently, discrepancies between observed and predicted mixture responses could reflect either a genuine interaction between compounds or limitations inherited from the single-compound model.

This issue is particularly important because imidacloprid is expected to drive much of the mixture toxicity owing to its substantially greater potency. Improving the description of its concentration–response pattern, especially at intermediate and high exposure levels, would therefore strengthen the interpretation of mixture predictions. Additional data around the transition between moderate and strong effects, together with a more explicit representation of high-concentration processes such as starvation (Jager et al., 2023) and mortality, could reduce this uncertainty.

Despite these limitations, generating mixture predictions from independently calibrated DEB-TKTD models would remain informative. It would test whether the integration of compound-specific internal exposure and time-dependent physiological stress provides a better description of mixture effects than approaches based solely on external concentrations. If the observed mixture responses were adequately reproduced, this would suggest that explicit interaction mechanisms are not required. Conversely, systematic deviations from the independent prediction could motivate the development of interaction terms acting on toxicokinetics, damage dynamics, stress intensity, or DEB energetic fluxes.

12.5 Conclusion

This study developed a DEB-TKTD framework for Aporrectodea caliginosa by coupling a previously calibrated DEB model with compound-specific toxicokinetic models and experimental growth and reproduction data. Recalibration of the baseline DEB model against control treatments improved the representation of untreated organisms and reduced the risk of attributing pre-existing physiological discrepancies to pesticide effects.

The models captured the main concentration-dependent responses to imidacloprid and epoxiconazole and confirmed their contrasting toxicities. Imidacloprid showed a much lower internal no-effect concentration and was best described by combined effects on assimilation and growth. For epoxiconazole, several physiological modes of action received similar support, although the assimilation and reproduction model had the lowest BIC. These pMoAs represent the energetic processes most consistent with the observed responses but do not identify the underlying biochemical mechanisms directly.

Remaining deviations reflected limitations in the representation of high-concentration imidacloprid kinetics, mortality, and the identification of epoxiconazole effects. Additional data near the no-effect concentrations and across the complete life cycle would improve parameter estimation. The framework nevertheless provides a mechanistic basis for predicting mixture effects by combining compound-specific stress responses and evaluating whether explicit interaction mechanisms are required.
