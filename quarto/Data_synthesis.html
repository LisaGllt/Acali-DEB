Synthesis of available data

Data obtained during Sylvain Bart PhD

A significant part of the data are from the PhD of Sylvain Bart [@bart_2018].

The first part of the data are from its article on Aporrectodea caliginosa life-cycle [@bart_2019d] where he studied the impact of food and density on growth and reproduction. The second part comes from the study of the impact of the Swing Gold pesticide on growth and reproduction [@bart_2020a]. The control data from this study can be used to inform a DEB model.

Earthworms, soil and food used in all Sylvain Bart experiments

Earthworm culture [@bart_2019d] :

  • Origin of the first earthworms : Adults of Aporrectodea caliginosa s.s. from an agriculture field at INRA (Estrées-mons, 49°52′ N 3°01′E) (refered later as the Mons culture)
  • Earthworms are cultured in a climate room at 15 ± 1 °C
  • Soil : a Luvisol (FAO soil classification), sampled from the top 0–20 cm in a permanent meadow in Versailles (48°48′ N, 2°5′ E) (refered later as Closeaux soil).
  • Food : horse dung which was frozen and defrosted twice and then milled (< 1 mm) as presented in Lowe and Butt (2005).

Experiments from @bart_2019d

  • Soil : Renewed every 28 days and maintained at 70% of the water holding capacity (WHC) (corresponding to 28% of water content).
  • Food : The horse dung at 70% of the water holding capacity and mixed with the soil.
  • Vessels : 1 L plastic vessels with 400 g equivalent dry soil.
  • Temperature : 15 ± 1 °C
  • Growth measurements every 14 days until they reached 500 g and then every 7 days to monitor the switch to adult stage

Experiment 1

Monitoring of the growth of 10 earthworms under ad libitum condition : 3 g/ind/14 days of horse dung milled at < 1mm. Earthworms are individually placed in a 1 L vessel.

This experiment can be separated in 2 experiments :

  • XP1_1 (?@fig-XP11) : The growth of earthworms individually placed in 1L vessels. These earthworms could not reproduce when adult.
  • XP1_2 (?@fig-XP12) : The growth of adult earthworms pooled first by 2 for 84 days (3 vessels with 2 individuals, for each food condition), then by 3 for 84 days (2 vessels with 3 individuals, for each food condition), and finally by 6 (all in- dividuals in one vessel, for each food condition) for 84 days. (corresponding to the first part of experiment 6 in @bart_2019d).

Usefull data from litterature

An estimate of cocoon energy content was required to parameterize the reproduction in the DEB model for A. caliginosa. Direct measurements of cocoon energy density are not available for this species. We therefore relied on published calorimetric data for Lumbricidae reported by @cummins_1971, who measured the energy density of whole individuals (“entire animals of various stages”). Mean energy densities were 5,690 cal g⁻¹ dry weight (n = 3), corresponding to 19,098 J g-1 dry weight, and 782 cal g-1 wet weight (n = 1), corresponding to 3,268 J g-1 wet weight. Although these measurements were not performed on cocoons specifically, they provide an order-of-magnitude estimate for annelid biomass energy density. Assuming a mean cocoon fresh mass of 10 mg, this corresponds to an energy content of approximately 33 J per cocoon (based on wet weight energy density). Comparable energy densities have been reported for soil invertebrates in other calorimetric studies (e.g. @driver_1974, @eggleton_2004), supporting the use of this approximation for the model prior on the energy of an egg.
